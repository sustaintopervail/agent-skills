# Upwork Proposal Writer

Writes job-winning Upwork proposals using eight proven persuasion rules, adapted to who will read the proposal. Every claim must be true and checkable on the freelancer's profile.

## Step 1: Read the job before writing

Read the full job post (through an Upwork connector if one is available, otherwise from what the user pasted). Note:
- The client's pain: what goes wrong for them today.
- Required and nice-to-have skills, budget range, hours, contract length.
- Hidden instructions, such as "write X on the first line". These are filters: obey them exactly, on the first line if asked.
- Screening questions. Each one needs its own answer.
- Who will read it. A non-technical owner, or a lead developer or technical screener?
- The client's name or product, only if a review or the post actually states it. Never guess a name.
- Preferred qualifications the freelancer misses (for example Job Success Score). Tell the freelancer before submitting.

## Step 2: Choose the style for the reader

**Non-technical owner (small business, "keep my site running")**: use the full sales style.
- Short, choppy sentences, 1 to 3 per paragraph.
- ALL CAPS on a few key words (Upwork has no bold).
- A few strategic emojis to break up the text.

**Technical reader (lead developer reviews, screening questions, "clear written English" required)**: keep the persuasion structure but drop caps, emojis and the P.S. offer. Plain, specific and short reads as competence to an engineer.

## Step 3: The persuasion rules

1. **Open with their pain, not your name.** Never "Hi, my name is" or "I am applying for". The first sentence names the problem they are living with. Focus on YOU and YOUR. In sales style, put an emoji in the first sentence.
2. **Become the solution.** Transition with a line like "I've mastered [service] so well that:" and follow with 3 bullets of tangible outcomes, not tasks.
3. **Paint the dream.** The bullets describe their life after hiring you: what stops breaking, how fast things get fixed, how they hear from you.
4. **Lead with proof.** Right after the bullets, put one real five-star client quote. In sales style prefix it with five star emojis. Pick the review closest to this job's skill.
5. **Show meals, not groceries.** Never list skills bare. Tie each skill to what it gives the client: stability, peace of mind, no repeat bugs.
6. **Make it an easy yes.** Offer a small, low-risk first step: a short trial on one real task. If the client already offers a paid trial, accept theirs instead of inventing one.
7. **Direct call to action.** One clear next step, for example: Reply with "LET'S TALK" and I can begin as early as tomorrow.
8. **Gentle urgency.** A P.S. with a small bonus for starting soon. Sales style only.

## Step 4: Honesty rules (never break these)

- **Reviews**: quote only real reviews, word for word, including the client's typos, so the client can find them on the profile. Trimming a sentence off the start is fine; rewording is not. If unsure a quote is real, ask.
- **Stats and badges**: use only what the profile shows today. If Job Success Score or Top Rated status has lapsed, don't claim it; use real totals instead (contracts, hours, earnings).
- **Free work**: Upwork bans unpaid test work. A free trial is fine only inside a contract: the client opens it and the freelancer doesn't bill the first hours. Say "open the contract and the first 2 hours are on me", never "free trial before we start".
- **NDA work**: never name or link a client project under NDA. Describe it generically ("a production SaaS app, a dynamic QR code builder") and say it's under NDA. If unsure the NDA allows even that, leave it out.
- **Assumed details**: any detail the freelancer didn't state (a feature, a check, a number) must be confirmed before submitting.
- **Links**: no external links in the profile overview. In a proposal, link only public code or work the freelancer owns.

## Step 5: Screening answers

- Answer each question directly in the first sentence.
- Use a real story from the freelancer: symptom, how they found the cause, the fix, a measured result.
- "What do you check first" questions: give the order of checks, evidence first, then the likely cause, then the fix and the prevention.
- Hours questions: state only hours the freelancer confirmed. Convert the client's time zone to the freelancer's and note daylight-saving changes.
- "Link to code" questions: link public work, or explain that client code is private and point to public work instead.

## Step 6: Rate and connects

- Bid inside the client's budget. Above the top of the range usually gets filtered out.
- Prefer slightly above the average bid when the record is strong; going well below it reads as junior and locks in a low rate on long contracts.
- Show the connects cost and the balance left before submitting.
- Boost only when a top slot is affordable and the client is worth it.

## Step 7: Before submitting

Show the freelancer the complete proposal: rate, connects cost, boost, attachments, cover letter and every screening answer. Ask about attachments. Submit only after an explicit yes. Never send it on assumption.

## Length

Cover letter under about 200 words. Screening answers 2 to 6 sentences each.

## Example

**Job:** "Ongoing WordPress maintenance, plugin updates and bug fixes, part-time, $15-35/hr." Posted by a small-business owner, so sales style.

**Bad start:** "Hi, my name is Sam and I am applying for your job. I have 10 years of experience in WordPress, PHP, JavaScript and MySQL."

**Good start:**

> 🔧 Jordan, YOUR site shouldn't break every time a plugin updates.
>
> And YOU shouldn't have to chase a freelancer for days to get it fixed.
>
> I've mastered ongoing WordPress maintenance so well that:
>
> ✅ YOUR plugins stay UPDATED, tested first so nothing breaks live
> ✅ Bugs get FIXED fast, usually the same day you report them
> ✅ YOU always know what's happening. CLEAR updates, no chasing
>
> ⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️ "[a real review from the profile, word for word]"

For a technical job with a lead-developer reviewer, the same structure becomes plain sentences with no caps, emojis or P.S.

## Common mistakes

- Opening with your name or "I am applying for".
- Skipping a "write X on the first line" instruction.
- Quoting a review that isn't on the profile, or rewording one.
- Claiming a lapsed badge or Job Success Score.
- Offering free work outside a contract.
- Naming a client project that's under NDA.
- Using caps and emojis on a proposal a developer will screen.
